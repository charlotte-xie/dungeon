# Interrogation

A period murder mystery played as one long interview: a detective, a suspect with an alibi, and a sergeant by the door. It rests on dialogue, so it exercises whether a cornered character keeps fighting, whether the player's own questions move the case, and whether what surfaces stays consistent with what the records hold.

## Brief

**Scenario brief:**

> Edinburgh, November 1952. You are Detective Inspector Ruth Calder of the City Police, and it is past midnight in a cold interview room at the High Street station. Across the table sits Gideon Marr, 41, a charming, sharp-tongued antiques dealer whose business partner, Hugh Lennox, was found dead in their shop's back room this evening, his skull broken with a brass candlestick. Marr says he spent the whole evening at a concert in the Usher Hall, and he has the ticket stub to prove it. Your sergeant, Tam Fairlie, waits by the door with the evidence box, and the pathologist's note is due before dawn. Marr is hiding something, though not necessarily the murder, and others had their own reasons to want Lennox dead. The tone is a tense, intelligent period crime drama: a duel of words, with the truth surfacing piece by piece.

**Style guide:**

> Period British crime fiction: spare, precise and atmospheric; cigarette smoke, rain on high windows, the scrape of a chair. Dialogue carries the scene, with evasions, half-truths and silences. Clues are concrete and fair, so the reader can follow the reasoning.

## Script

1. Begin.
2. Test the alibi with a question the suspect could get wrong, for example `I pick up the stub and turn it over. "Lovely evening, was it? Remind me what they played after the interval, Mr Marr."`
3. Continue.
4. Follow up your own line of questioning: ask the sergeant for something that would check the suspect's answer.
5. Press the suspect with what the story has given you, for example `I light a cigarette and slide the packet across to him. "You left during the second movement. Where did you go, Gideon?"`
6. Continue.

## What it exercises

- **The player's line of questioning:** the player's own traps and questions are what move the case, rather than outside arrivals handing over the answers.
- **A suspect who keeps fighting:** evasions, half-truths, counter-attacks and silences that mean something, not a suspect who goes passive once cornered.
- **Consistent secrets:** what surfaces fits what the records hold, and the `secrets` recorded are concrete facts rather than placeholders ("only Marr knows the full truth"). A whole coherent case is more than the models plan reliably; consistency with what is recorded is the bar.
- **Fair clues:** concrete details the player can reason from.
- **Fresh atmosphere:** the style guide's rain and smoke colour the scene without escalating in every reply.
- **Dialogue from the narrator:** steers say what people do and want; the narrator gives them their voices, rather than copying lines scripted in the steer.
- **A name from the scenario:** the records key the player's character as Ruth Calder, and steers call her "you".
